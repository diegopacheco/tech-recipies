# Director of Engineering

## What is a Director of Engineering?

A **Director of Engineering** is a **manager of managers**. You stop managing engineers
and start managing engineering managers (plus a few senior/staff ICs). You stop
executing a plan and start setting one. LeadDev calls the director "the glue keeping
engineering together."

The director sits *between* strategy and execution: the CTO/VP sets overall strategy, the
EM leads a team into the granular details, and the director translates strategy into what
each team actually does each quarter and owns that it lands. It is fundamentally an
**operational depth** role — running teams well inside an existing structure.

## What you do in this role

- **Run a group of teams through their managers** (typically 2–6 teams, 20–60+ engineers).
- **Grow and hire managers.** Your leverage now comes from the quality of the leaders
  under you, not your own hands on a keyboard.
- **Own delivery, team health, and headcount** across the group; troubleshoot process
  bottlenecks and own the operational hygiene.
- **Translate company strategy into quarterly team plans** and report up on progress,
  risk, and what you need to go faster.
- **Align cross-functionally** with product, design, data, security, and compliance.
- **Own budget, staffing plans, metrics, and delivery systems** for your area.

## Where it sits / how companies level it

Reports to a VP/Head of Engineering (or CTO in smaller orgs). Direct reports are mostly
EMs plus some Staff/Principal ICs — usually 3–6 of them. Two steps removed from ICs and
rarely writing production code. 2025 US market data: median base ~$190K–$215K (vs. ~$245K
for VP).

## Pros

- **Real organizational leverage** — you shape how multiple teams operate, not one.
- **Strategy meets execution.** You own outcomes big enough to matter but concrete
  enough to steer.
- **Clear path toward VP/Head**, with executive exposure.
- **You build the leaders** who build the teams — durable, compounding impact.

## Cons

- **Two steps from the code.** If you loved building, that's basically gone.
- **Accountable for outcomes you influence only indirectly**, through other managers.
- **Operational grind:** budgets, compliance, staffing, process — the unglamorous plumbing.
- **Squeezed between VP strategy and EM reality** with no place to hide.

## What is good about the role

- **You fix systems, not tickets.** A structural change you make improves delivery for
  dozens of people at once.
- **You develop leaders.** Watching an EM you coached grow into a great manager is a
  distinct, lasting reward.
- **Broad influence** across the org and a seat where strategy actually gets shaped into action.
- **The org runs better because you exist** — you're the connective tissue that keeps
  teams aligned.

## What is bad about the role (the politics and the snakes)

- **The blame magnet.** When any team in your group misses, it's your number that's red —
  even when the root cause was a bad requirement handed down or a report who hid a problem.
- **Peer-director turf wars.** Directors compete for headcount, scope, and the interesting
  charters. Some will quietly expand into your area, undercut your plans in leadership
  meetings, or hoard resources — smiling the whole time.
- **You inherit other people's messes.** A weak EM, a toxic senior IC, an underwater
  project — all now yours to fix, often ones you didn't create.
- **Reorg roulette.** Directors are the layer most reshuffled in reorgs; your scope can be
  redrawn overnight by politics above you.
- **Managing up is now half the job.** Your VP's perception of you is shaped in rooms you're
  not in; if you don't actively manage that narrative, someone else will shape it for you.
- **You enforce decisions you didn't make** and can't always explain honestly — layoffs,
  reorgs, dropped projects land on you to deliver to the teams.
