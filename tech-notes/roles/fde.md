# Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)

## What is an FDE?

A **Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE)** is a software engineer who embeds directly
with a customer to make a vendor's product actually solve that customer's hardest
problems. Instead of building one capability that serves many customers (a
traditional "Dev"), an FDE builds many capabilities for a single customer.

The role was invented and popularized by **Palantir**, where it is called a
Forward Deployed Software Engineer (FDSE), internally a "Delta" (as opposed to a
"Dev"). Palantir's own framing:

- FDEs are measured by **customer outcomes**, not lines of code or features shipped.
- They combine **product knowledge, engineering, product customization, and
  problem-solving** for one customer.
- They differ from a traditional consultant: **they write production code**, not slides.

Gergely Orosz (The Pragmatic Engineer) captures the tension well:

> The FDE role looks like it's two roles rolled into one: working for the customer,
> and also contributing to their employer's core product.

That dual mandate — solve the customer's problem *and* feed learnings back into the
core product — is the essence of the role. The FDE is the feedback loop between the
messy real world and the product roadmap.

Because the term is new and hot, it is already contested. There is real overlap and
confusion with **Solutions Engineer, Sales Engineer, Solutions Architect, System
Integrator (SI), and Technical Customer Success**. The distinguishing line is that
an FDE ships production code inside the customer's environment.

## What you do in this role

- **Embed with the customer.** Sit with their teams, learn the domain (banking,
  healthcare, defense, logistics, semiconductors, life sciences) and their messy
  data architecture.
- **Discover where value actually is.** Especially with AI/LLMs, most enterprises
  want the technology but don't know *where* inside the company it should go. The
  FDE finds those opportunities.
- **Architect and build production solutions** on top of the vendor's platform /
  models — real code that runs in the customer's environment, not demos.
- **Iterate fast, hand-in-hand with the customer.** Tight feedback loops mean high
  impact in short cycles.
- **Feed the core product.** Turn recurring, customer-specific pain into
  generalizable features that go back into the vendor's product.
- **Wear many hats:** software development, data engineering, ML/LLM deployment,
  customer engagement, and organizational/political navigation.
- **Travel.** On-site work is common — typically 20–50% travel depending on the
  company (OpenAI postings cite up to 50%; Palantir ~25%).

## What Silicon Valley companies are doing this

The role went from niche to the most fought-over job in AI. Job postings for FDEs
jumped **~800%+ between January and September 2025**.

- **Palantir** — the origin. At one point had more FDSEs than traditional
  engineers; embedding engineers close to customers created near-unchurnable
  accounts and is credited as a core driver of its business results.
- **OpenAI** — began building an FDE team in late 2024, accelerated through 2025.
  Stood up a standalone **OpenAI Deployment Company** (backed by $4B+) and acquired
  applied-AI firm **Tomoro**, bringing ~150 FDEs/deployment specialists on day one.
  Hiring for verticals like semiconductors, life sciences, and startups.
- **Anthropic** — building FDE teams to deploy frontier models inside enterprises;
  also partnering with consultancies.
- **Google / Google Cloud** — posted dozens of FDE openings (reported ~59) with
  six-figure salaries.
- **Microsoft** — announced a **$2.5B investment** in a **6,000-person** FDE-style
  "Frontier" engineering org, plus partnerships with Global SIs (Accenture,
  Capgemini, EY, KPMG, PwC).
- **AWS** — announced a **$1B investment** into a new Forward Deployed Engineering
  organization.
- **Salesforce** and many startups — adopting the model to drive adoption of agents
  and AI products.

**Compensation** runs high: reported OpenAI FDEs at **$350K–$550K** total comp at
mid-to-senior levels; Anthropic ranging **$300K–$1.2M** depending on level. Hiring
spans SF, Seattle, NYC, Washington DC (government-adjacent), and international hubs
(London, Munich, Paris, Singapore, Tokyo, Sydney, Dublin).

## Pros

- **High impact, fast.** Tight customer feedback loops let you ship something very
  impactful in a very short time.
- **Creates a business moat.** Embedded engineers make accounts sticky and hard to
  churn — Palantir turned complex, high-risk deployments into long-term, high-value,
  near-unchurnable relationships.
- **Scales institutional understanding, not just software.** Converts real-world
  domain complexity into durable product infrastructure.
- **Feeds the product roadmap.** Real customer pain becomes generalized features —
  the FDE is a live discovery channel for the core product.
- **Broad, varied, high-growth work** across many industries and problem domains.
- **Highly paid and in extreme demand** right now.
- **Rare, valuable skill profile:** engineers who can write production code *and*
  navigate institutional politics/domain complexity are hard to find and hard to
  train — which is exactly why the role commands a premium.

## Cons

- **Burnout risk.** Intense technical work + client-facing pressure + heavy travel
  (20–50%) is a known burnout driver.
- **Poorly defined role.** Companies often mislabel a solutions engineer or project
  manager as an FDE without the deep engineering chops, leading to failed
  engagements and turnover.
- **Doesn't scale cleanly.** The "technical customer success" flavor works under ~20
  customers with moderate complexity; past that, deal volume and implementation
  complexity outpace the talent you can hire and retain.
- **Can mask product flaws.** Critics argue the model fosters customer dependency on
  the vendor's engineers, hiding underlying product gaps and sustaining high costs.
- **Lower early-stage margins.** Deep customization means accepting lower gross
  margins while building market position (contrast mature SaaS like ServiceNow ~79%
  / Workday ~75% gross margins).
- **Title dilution (the SeattleDataGuy thesis).** As AI vendors chase enterprise
  adoption "at any cost," the FDE label is broadening. Microsoft already routes FDE
  work through Global SIs (Accenture, Capgemini, EY, KPMG, PwC). The prediction:
  FDEs will fracture into two tiers — those who dive deep, understand the business,
  and produce outcomes generalizable across an industry, and those who are really
  just doing one-off implementation/integration work (i.e., the new SIs/contractors).
  The risk: if you outsource the FDE role to partners, the feedback loop that
  improves your core product may not survive — they build one-off solutions, not
  general product.

## What is good about the role

- **You see your work matter, immediately.** You are next to the person whose job you
  are changing. There is no product manager or three-team handoff between you and the
  outcome — you build it, they use it that afternoon.
- **You learn a whole industry, not just a stack.** Banking, defense, healthcare,
  semiconductors — you leave each engagement fluent in a domain most engineers never
  touch. That compounds into rare, high-leverage career capital.
- **Range.** You bounce across data engineering, backend, ML/LLM, architecture, and
  human problems. It's the opposite of maintaining one service for three years.
- **Proximity to power and decisions.** You sit with executives and operators, see how
  real businesses actually run and fail, and get exposure that pure ICs rarely get.
- **Leverage into anything next.** The pattern-matching across many companies makes FDEs
  strong founders, product leaders, and senior engineers. You've seen 20 versions of the
  same problem.
- **You are the product's conscience.** When you carry real customer pain back to the
  roadmap and it ships, you shaped the product for everyone — not just one account.
- **Paid extremely well right now**, with more demand than qualified supply.

## What is bad about the role (the politics and the snakes)

The technical difficulty is rarely the hard part. The hard part is people, and you
should go in eyes-open:

- **You serve two masters.** You work *for the customer* and *for your employer's
  product* at the same time. When those pull apart (customer wants a one-off hack; your
  company wants a generalizable feature) you're stuck in the middle, and both sides think
  you owe them.
- **Internal politics on the customer side — the snakes.** You are an outsider walking
  into someone else's org chart. Some people there will see you as a threat: the AI/vendor
  that makes their team look slow, exposes that their pet system doesn't work, or
  automates away their headcount. They will withhold access, feed you bad data, take
  credit for your wins, and blame you for anything that breaks. Your champion who invited
  you in can get reorged out, and suddenly you have no cover. Reading who actually holds
  power vs. who has the title is a survival skill.
- **You get blamed for the product's flaws.** When the platform can't do something, you
  are the face standing in the room. The customer's frustration lands on you, not on the
  distant product team that shipped the gap.
- **Success is fuzzy and political.** "Customer outcomes" is a soft metric that others can
  reinterpret. A deployment can be technically excellent and still be called a failure
  because a stakeholder wanted it to fail, or wanted the budget elsewhere.
- **Scope creep and being a human shield.** You get pulled into work far outside
  engineering — training, hand-holding, firefighting, selling internally on the customer's
  behalf — because you're the one who's actually there.
- **Burnout is structural, not incidental.** Travel, on-site pressure, being "on" in front
  of clients constantly, and carrying the emotional load of the relationship add up fast.
- **Dilution risk to your résumé.** As the title spreads to every implementation and SI
  role, "FDE" starts meaning less. The market will split into deep FDEs and glorified
  integrators — and you want to make sure your work lands you in the first bucket.
- **You can become a crutch that hides the product.** If the customer only succeeds because
  *you* are there, that's job security today and a fragile, un-scalable position tomorrow —
  for you and for the vendor.
