# Engineering Manager (EM)

## What is an EM?

An **Engineering Manager** is the person accountable for a single engineering team:
its people, its delivery, and its health. It is the first real management rung — you
stop being measured by what *you* ship and start being measured by what *your team*
ships and becomes.

Charity Majors' framing is the canonical one. Management is not a promotion, it's a
*change of profession*. The two crafts pull in opposite directions: being a good
engineer means blocking out interruptions to focus deeply; being a good manager means
being available and interrupt-driven. You can't do both well at the same instant.

Her **Engineer/Manager Pendulum**: the best technical leaders swing back and forth
between IC and manager roles over a career. The best frontline EMs are never more than
2–3 years removed from hands-on work. Take a management role for a minimum of ~2 years
(so the team gets stability) but ideally no more than ~5 if you want to keep technical
credibility.

## What you do in this role

- **Manage people:** 1:1s, career growth, feedback, performance, hiring, and firing.
- **Own delivery:** sprint planning, unblocking, prioritization, and shipping.
- **Be interruptible.** Your job is to absorb interruptions so your engineers don't
  have to; hand off the challenging assignments so they can grow.
- **Stay technical enough to be dangerous.** Evaluate work independently, shape
  technical strategy, judge feasibility and tradeoffs, and resolve technical conflicts.
  Without technical grounding, the whole team's judgment degrades.
- **Translate up and down:** turn company strategy into team work, and report progress,
  risk, and needs back up.
- **Protect the team:** from thrash, from politics, from unrealistic demands.
- **Grow the humans.** A team deserves a manager who actually *wants* the people-leadership job.

## Where it sits / how companies level it

Reports to a Director or Head/VP of Engineering. Typically owns one team of ~4–8
engineers. At most tech companies EM is roughly parallel in level to Senior/Staff on
the IC track — the fork in the ladder, not a rung above it. Many companies (Honeycomb,
GitLab, etc.) publish explicit EM "bill of rights and responsibilities" to keep the
role honest.

## Pros

- **Multiplier effect.** A good EM makes 6–8 people meaningfully better — leverage a
  single IC can't match.
- **Career optionality.** Opens the path to Director/VP, and the pendulum lets you go back.
- **You shape culture and careers**, not just code.
- **Still close to the work.** Frontline EMs stay near the technology and the team.

## Cons

- **It's a different job you may not have signed up for.** Many great engineers hate it.
- **Loss of maker time and flow.** The deep-work high mostly goes away.
- **Skill atrophy risk.** Stay too long and your technical edge dulls.
- **You absorb stress and rarely get credit.** Wins are the team's; problems are yours.

## What is good about the role

- **You see people grow because of you** — someone gets promoted, gains confidence,
  ships the thing they didn't think they could. That's a reward ICs rarely get.
- **You fix the environment, not just the bug.** You can remove the friction that was
  slowing five people down.
- **Broad relationships.** You work across product, design, and other teams and build
  organizational range.
- **A genuine multiplier of impact** when done well.

## What is bad about the role (the politics and the snakes)

- **You're in the middle, always.** Leadership pushes dates down; engineers push reality
  up. You are the shock absorber, and both sides can resent you for it.
- **Credit-stealing and blame-shifting.** Peers who "manage up" well can claim your
  team's wins in the rooms you're not in; a bad skip-level can scapegoat you for their
  own bad calls.
- **Performance management is genuinely hard and lonely.** PIPs, layoffs, and firing
  people you like — you carry that, usually in silence.
- **Politics is now part of the job.** Headcount, promotions, and roadmap are won in
  rooms, not on merit alone. Refusing to play means your team loses resources.
- **You can't hide behind output.** When there's nothing to "ship" on a hard week, it's
  easy to feel useless — the value is real but invisible.
- **The stress rolls downhill onto you and stops.** Protecting the team means eating the
  chaos yourself.
