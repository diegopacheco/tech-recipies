# Architect (Software / Solution / Enterprise)

## What is an Architect?

**Software Architect** is an umbrella term for the people who own the *shape* of technical
systems rather than the day-to-day code. It splits into three common flavors, and knowing
which one is meant matters:

- **Technical Architect** — deep in one technology stack or platform; owns the technical
  design and standards for a system.
- **Solution Architect** — owns *how* a specific business problem gets solved end to end:
  requirements, blueprint, tech stack, and staying involved through delivery. Bridges the
  gap between concept and implementation.
- **Enterprise Architect** — owns the org-wide IT landscape: *what* to do across the whole
  company, the technology roadmap, frameworks, and standards that guide investments.

The clean one-liner: **the enterprise architect defines *what* to do, the solution architect
decides *how* to do it, and the technical architect owns the *how* within one system.**

## What you do in this role

- **Gather requirements from stakeholders** — business owners, executives, customers — and
  turn pain points into technical requirements.
- **Design the blueprint:** architecture, technical specs, tech-stack decisions, and the
  path from concept to production.
- **Communicate the vision** to dev teams via conceptual models, diagrams, RFCs, and design docs.
- **Stay involved through the lifecycle** — manage timelines, mitigate technical risk, and
  guard quality attributes (scalability, maintainability, robustness, security).
- **Set standards and frameworks** (especially enterprise architects) that guide projects
  across the business.
- **Make and defend tradeoffs** between competing constraints, cost, and time.

## Where it sits / how companies level it

Architect can be an IC "Staff-plus" title (the Architect archetype in Will Larson's Staff
Engineer framework) or a distinct track with its own ladder. Solution/technical architects
usually sit inside or alongside engineering teams; enterprise architects sit closer to the
CTO/IT-strategy layer. Reporting varies widely — often into a Chief Architect, VP, or CTO.

## Pros

- **You own the big technical picture** without the people-management overhead of a director.
- **High-leverage decisions.** One good architecture choice pays off across many teams and years.
- **Breadth.** You see the whole system and how the pieces fit — a rare vantage point.
- **Respected senior technical role** with a clear path to Chief Architect.

## Cons

- **Risk of the "ivory tower."** Architects who stop coding lose touch and get ignored.
- **Influence without authority.** You design; other teams implement — or don't.
- **You own the consequences of decisions made under uncertainty**, sometimes years later.
- **Can become a bottleneck** if every design must route through you.

## What is good about the role

- **You get to think in systems** — the deep, satisfying work of making many parts fit
  cleanly and last.
- **Long shelf life for your decisions.** A good architecture outlives dozens of feature
  releases; you shape the codebase for years.
- **Cross-org perspective.** You're one of the few people who actually understands how the
  whole thing hangs together.
- **Technical leadership without managing people** — the appeal of the Staff/Architect track.

## What is bad about the role (the politics and the snakes)

- **All accountability, no line authority.** You're blamed when a system fails to scale, but
  you couldn't force the teams to build it your way — they can quietly ignore your design and
  leave you holding the outcome.
- **"Architecture" is where egos fight.** Design reviews and standards become proxy wars;
  rival architects and senior engineers relitigate your decisions to protect their turf or
  their favorite technology.
- **The ivory-tower accusation is a weapon.** The moment you're seen as too removed from code,
  teams dismiss your input — and political rivals will happily frame you that way.
- **You're a convenient scapegoat.** Because you touch everything and own the "big decisions,"
  failures anywhere get pinned on the architecture (and on you), even when execution was the
  real problem.
- **Enterprise politics and vendor games.** Enterprise architects get pulled into procurement
  fights, vendor lobbying, and executive pet-technology mandates that override sound design.
- **Slow, invisible wins vs. fast, visible blame.** Good architecture prevents problems that
  never happen — hard to get credit for — while any visible failure is loud and immediate.
