# Head of Engineering

## What is a Head of Engineering?

**Head of Engineering** is the flexible, context-dependent top-of-engineering title,
most common at startups and pre-IPO companies that haven't locked in their org
structure. It signals "the person running engineering" without committing to the VP or
CTO framing — which is exactly why founders like it.

In practice a Head of Engineering leans **operational and tactical**: keeping projects on
track, maintaining technical standards, and staying close to day-to-day team management
(sometimes still in code reviews, sprint planning, and technical decisions). A VP of
Engineering, by contrast, leans strategic — long-term goals, org systems, executive/board
work. The Head is often the acting CTO, the CTO's sidekick, or a role that *grows into*
VP/CTO as the company scales.

## What you do in this role

- **Run engineering end to end** at the current company size — delivery, quality, and team.
- **Stay hands-close.** At smaller scale you're still in technical decisions, architecture
  calls, and sometimes the code.
- **Manage team leads / EMs** and own hiring, standards, and process.
- **Be the bridge to the founders/CEO** on what engineering can and can't do.
- **Set up the scaffolding** — process, hiring, technical direction — that a future VP/CTO
  structure will formalize.
- **Wear whatever hat is missing** that week; the role is defined by the gaps around it.

## Where it sits / how companies level it

Usually the most senior engineering leader in a startup, reporting to the CEO or CTO. Scope
ranges enormously — from leading one team of 8 to running all of engineering at a 100-person
company. The title's whole value is flexibility: it can grow into CTO or VP as the org
matures, or sit alongside a founder-CTO as the execution counterpart.

## Pros

- **Broad ownership early.** You run the whole show while it's small enough to actually hold
  in your head.
- **Stay technical *and* lead.** Uniquely, you often keep hands-on while owning strategy.
- **High growth optionality.** The role can become CTO or VP as the company scales.
- **Direct line to founders** and real influence on the product and company.

## Cons

- **Undefined and overloaded.** "Head of everything not yet hired" — scope creep is the default.
- **Title ambiguity.** Externally it reads as junior to VP/CTO, which can hurt later.
- **Stretched thin** across strategy, delivery, hiring, and firefighting simultaneously.
- **You may get leapfrogged.** Companies sometimes hire a VP/CTO *above* the Head as they scale.

## What is good about the role

- **You build the engineering org from scratch** — culture, hiring bar, architecture, process
  are yours to set. Few roles offer that blank canvas.
- **Close to both the code and the founders** — you rarely have to choose between technical
  depth and real influence.
- **Fast, visible impact.** At startup scale a decision you make on Monday changes how the
  team works by Friday.
- **A launchpad.** Do it well and you grow into CTO/VP with the org.

## What is bad about the role (the politics and the snakes)

- **The "will they hire above me?" anxiety.** As the company raises money, investors and the
  CEO may want a "proven" VP/CTO — and you can be demoted-in-place or boxed in by someone
  brought over the top. This is the signature political risk of the title.
- **Founder dynamics are the real politics.** A founder-CTO who won't let go, or a
  non-technical CEO who overrides engineering on a whim, can undermine you daily — and you
  have little formal cover.
- **No structure to protect you.** With undefined scope, blame is easy to assign and hard to
  deflect; when anything technical goes wrong, you're the obvious target.
- **You're the buffer for everything.** Hiring, fires, outages, investor tech due-diligence,
  a co-founder's pet project — all funnel through you, often with no one to delegate to.
- **Reward can lag responsibility.** You do VP/CTO work under a smaller title and comp until
  the company decides to "formalize" it — if it ever does.
- **Early-hire loyalty politics.** Managing founding engineers who feel they own the codebase
  (and have the founders' ear) is a minefield of egos and informal power.
