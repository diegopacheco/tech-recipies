# VP of Engineering

## What is a VP of Engineering?

A **VP of Engineering** owns the **entire engineering function** and the machine that runs
it. Where a director owns depth (running teams well inside a structure), the VP owns
**breadth and systems**: how teams are structured, how work flows, how decisions get made,
and how all of it scales. They usually report to the CTO or CEO and sit at the executive
table.

A useful contrast: the **CTO** is often the outward/technology-strategy and visionary role;
the **VP of Engineering** is the inward execution-and-organization role that makes the
engineering org predictable and humane at scale. When the bottleneck isn't any one team but
the *system connecting all of them*, that's a VP problem.

## What you do in this role

- **Build the operating system of engineering:** org design, planning cadence, decision
  rights, delivery systems.
- **Own the hiring engine, the budget, and the headcount plan** for all of engineering.
- **Protect the team's energy and momentum** as the org scales past founder-mode heroics.
- **Set engineering strategy** and cascade it down through directors to teams.
- **Sit at the executive table** — connect engineering to the business, the board, and
  peer functions (product, sales, finance).
- **Grow directors and senior leaders**; you're three-plus steps from the code.

## Where it sits / how companies level it

Reports to CTO or CEO; leads directors (and through them, EMs and ICs). Owns the whole
function — often dozens to hundreds of engineers. 2025 US market data: median base ~$245K
(roughly a 17% jump over director), plus significant equity. In startups the line between
VP, Head, and CTO is blurry and title-inflated.

## Pros

- **You own the whole function** — maximum organizational leverage in engineering.
- **Executive seat.** You shape company strategy, not just engineering's slice.
- **You design the machine** and see it run across the entire org.
- **Top-of-market compensation** and a path toward CTO/COO.

## Cons

- **Fully removed from the craft.** Your day is people, systems, and meetings.
- **Accountable for everything, in control of little directly.** Outcomes flow through
  many layers.
- **Politically exposed.** You live and die by executive-peer relationships.
- **First to go when things go wrong.** VP is a high-turnover seat.

## What is good about the role

- **You get to build the culture and the system** the whole org lives inside — the highest
  leverage most engineers will ever have.
- **You remove the structural pain** that no single team could fix, and thousands of hours
  of other people's frustration disappear.
- **A real voice in where the company goes**, not just how it builds.
- **You grow leaders who grow the org** — impact that compounds for years and outlasts you.

## What is bad about the role (the politics and the snakes)

- **Executive politics is now the job, not a distraction.** Your influence depends on
  alliances with the CPO, CFO, and CEO; a peer who plays the game better can starve
  engineering of budget and headcount regardless of merit.
- **You're the accountable name for everything.** An outage, a missed launch, attrition — it
  lands on you, even when the cause was a business decision forced on you from outside.
- **The CTO/VP boundary is a knife fight in many orgs.** Overlapping mandates, unclear
  ownership of "technology strategy," and ego make this one of the most politically fraught
  seats in the company.
- **You deliver decisions you didn't make and may not agree with** — layoffs, reorgs,
  strategy pivots — and must sell them convincingly downward while absorbing the anger.
- **Highest-turnover leadership seat.** VPs are frequently the sacrifice when the board wants
  to "signal change"; your tenure can end for reasons that have nothing to do with your work.
- **Isolation.** Fewer peers, guarded conversations, and everyone below you now filters what
  they tell you — you lose ground truth exactly when you need it most.
